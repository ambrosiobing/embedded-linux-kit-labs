# P19: a blocked lab, and the document that unblocked the question

The lab is [SPI LCD tilt meter](../chapters/19-spi-lcd-tilt-meter.md). It is
still blocked, but it is blocked on something it can name, which it could not do
a week ago.

## The problem

The 3.5 inch panel mates with the first 26 header pins through a socket on its
underside and carries no pass-through. So with the panel seated, pins 1, 3, 5 and
6 are underneath it. Pins 1 and 17 are the only 3.3 V pins on the whole header
and both are inside that covered range, so a sensor on that host has no supply
pin. Hardware I2C1 exists only on pins 3 and 5, so there is no second bus to move
to either.

One detail from the panel's own pinout made a fix conceivable rather than
impossible: it declares pins 3 and 5 not connected, so the bus is electrically
free and only physically buried.

## The question that decided it

Two routes existed. A stacking header raises the panel and restores every pin,
which is one small part this bin does not contain. Or the sensor takes 3.3 V from
the labelled rail of another lab, grounds above pin 26, and runs a bit-banged bus
on two free GPIOs, which costs nothing.

Everything turned on one question: **does the carrier board overhang pins 27 to
40?** If it does, the second route has nowhere to attach a jumper.

## The inference that was deliberately refused

An answer was available by reasoning. The vendor's wiki says the board is "same
size as your Raspberry Pi 3 / 4 / 5". The glass panel's dimension drawing gives
roughly 55 by 85 mm. A carrier cannot be smaller than the glass it carries, and
a Pi 3 Model B is 56 by 85 mm, so the board must run the full length of the
header.

**That chain reaches the right answer and it was not written down.** It rests on
a marketing phrase plus a dimension drawing of a different part, which is the
exact shape of reasoning behind two claims this volume has already withdrawn. It
would have been a true sentence resting on a false method, and the method is what
a reader inherits.

The chapter asked instead for a ruler along the carrier's long edge.

## The settlement, Thursday 8 October 2026

**No ruler was needed.** The carrier's own schematic answers it without any
measuring at all.

Its Raspberry Pi connector symbol is drawn as thirteen rows of two, numbered 1
and 2 down to **25 and 26**, and it stops there. The netlist carries pads 1
through 26 and nothing above. **Pins 27 to 40 are not contacts on this board and
are not listed even as not connected.** The carrier outline is 85.06 by 56.21 mm
against the Pi's 85 by 56 mm.

The vendor's connection note says the same thing in words: the Pi has 40 pins,
this screen has 26, and the socket is lined up with pins 1 to 26.

## What follows

Those fourteen pins are electrically untouched and mechanically buried the moment
the panel is seated. **The free route does not exist.** No overlay configuration
changes where a connector physically fits.

P19 needs a 2x20 stacking header, or a tall header whose pins 27 to 40 clear the
26-pin socket. That is not in this bin.

**That is a better position than before**, and worth saying plainly: the lab is
no longer blocked on something nobody knows. It is blocked on a purchase it can
name, which is a thing a reader can act on.

## The lesson, which is not about displays

The cheapest measurement turned out to be a document nobody had opened. A ruler
was asked for, correctly, because the available reasoning was not good enough.
Then the reasoning was not needed either, because the carrier's schematic had
been one click away the entire time.

Before planning a measurement, check whether the answer is already written down
somewhere nobody has looked.

## One note for a different board

This is the (A) carrier: 480 x 320, SPI, resistive touch, no pass-through. The
(B), (C) and (G) are different boards. The (G) schematic, dated Saturday
28 March 2025, draws a full 40-pin header and **still has no pass-through**, so
the same physical conclusion applies to it unless someone measures a cut-back
edge on that revision. Do not apply this page's conclusion to a board it was not
about.
