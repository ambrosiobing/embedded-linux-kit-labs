# P03: one conductor, three spellings, one vendor

The lab is [Cat-M MQTT node with GNSS stamp](../chapters/03-cat-m-mqtt-node-with-gnss-stamp.md).
Its record is mostly about a documentation hazard rather than an error, and it is
the reason a convention in this volume that looks like padding is not.

## The reading, Saturday 3 October 2026

This board was read after P02's withdrawal, to find out whether it made the same
claim. It does not. The SIM7070G leaves the draft's indicator pin alone, and
puts the module's power key on header pin 7, BCM GPIO4, exactly where the
SIM7020E puts it.

Two other things it does claim are worth knowing before wiring:

| Connection | Header pin | BCM | Condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| DTR | 37 | GPIO26 | **Only** with the DTR jumper in position A. Default is disconnected |
| IO level select | jumper | | Default 3.3 V. The other position makes the HAT's logic **5 V** |

That second row is the single connection the volume's electrical safety lab
exists to prevent, offered on this board as a two-position jumper. Never move it.

## The near miss

An intermediate version of the indicator correction had used BCM26. That is the
pin the DTR jumper connects in position A, and it is also a board-address pin on
the data acquisition HAT in another lab.

**It was abandoned for the second reason, found while writing a different
chapter, before anyone had read this board's pages properly.** The right answer
arrived partly by luck. The reading is what settles it now, and the luck is
recorded because a volume that only prints its successful reasoning teaches
readers to trust reasoning that happened to work.

## The part that generalises: one pin, three spellings

The module's own hardware design document settles only half the question. It
confirms which lines the module has to route, PWRKEY on module pin 1, DTR on 3,
RI on 4, DCD on 5, CTS on 7, RTS on 8, STATUS on 66 and NETLIGHT on 52.

**Which Raspberry Pi header pin each of those reaches is the carrier board's
decision, not the module's.** A module datasheet cannot answer it and the HAT's
own pages must. That distinction is worth internalising before reading any
vendor material: a module document and a carrier document answer different
questions, and confusing them produces claims that are individually sourced and
jointly wrong.

Then the spellings. The vendor's product page calls the power key pin "P4". The
same vendor's wiki calls it "P7 (wiringPi number)". Neither page says which
numbering scheme it is using. **They are the same conductor:** wiringPi 7 is BCM
GPIO4 is physical header pin 7.

A reader who assumes physical numbering on the first page wires the power key to
the 5 V rail.

This is why every pin in this volume is given as header pin and BCM number
together, in every table, every time. It reads as padding until the first time it
is not.

## Monday 20 October 2026, 16:15 to 17:00: the UART answers, and not from this HAT

The bearer question is still open and this sitting did not close it. What it
did establish is worth the entry, and one of the two findings is a mistake
made at the bench rather than a property of any part.

**Both modem HATs were on the one header.** The SIM7600E-H was seated and the
SIM7070G was stacked on top of it, which puts two module transmit pins on
GPIO15 and two `PWRKEY` lines on GPIO4. Two push-pull outputs driving one
input is contention, not merely ambiguity, and this chapter already carries
the rule as a pitfall in its own words. The Pi was powered down and the
SIM7600E-H removed as soon as the photographs showed it.

**The module that answered `/dev/serial0` was the SIM7600E-H.** That matters
more than the stacking, because the reading looked like a result. `AT` and
`ATE1` returned `OK` on the Pi's own UART, which is the route this chapter
documents and which had never been tested. Three readings together named the
responder. `AT+CMNB=?` returned `ERROR`, and a SIM7070G cannot refuse the
bearer selector it exists for. `AT+CNMP=?` returned
`(2,9,10,13,14,19,22,38,39,48,54,59,60,63,67)`, identical value for value to
what the SIM7600E-H had answered on `ttyUSB2` an hour earlier, and carrying
CDMA, EVDO and TD-SCDMA modes that a Cat-M module's table does not. And
`+CME ERROR: SIM not inserted`, from the HAT whose SIM had just been moved
into the other one. Had the bearer commands been run and written down from
that port, this volume would now record a SIM7070G refusing Cat-M, which is
false.

**So the UART route works and the chapter can stop calling the HAT's USB
cable the only way in.** `/dev/serial0` points at `ttyS0`, `enable_uart=1` is
in `config.txt` so the mini UART's baud is pinned to a fixed core clock,
no login shell holds the port, and ModemManager filters `ttyS0` as a
platform-driver port and does not compete for it. All four of those were
checked this evening.

**With the SIM7070G alone on the header it does not answer.** Keystrokes
reach the UART, confirmed with minicom's local echo, and nothing comes back,
at 115200. Everything that could be eliminated from the host side was:
`PWR` steady and `NET` blinking, so the module is running and on a network;
`enable_uart=1` present; no `ttyUSB*` and no getty, so nothing else holds the
port. What is left is the routing of the module's own UART, which on these
HATs is a jumper block choosing between the on-board USB bridge and the
40-pin header, and that block's legend has not been read. It is the next
thing to do and it is one photograph.

**The bench is left this way.** The SIM7070G alone on eplepi's header, the
Elisa prepaid in its holder, its LTE antenna on the `LTE` port and the `GNSS`
port empty. The SIM7600E-H is off the board, with no SIM. The
`elisa-lte` NetworkManager connection is defined and **down**, which is where
a roaming prepaid should be left; `sudo nmcli connection up elisa-lte` brings
it back, and it will want the SIM returned to the SIM7600E-H first.
