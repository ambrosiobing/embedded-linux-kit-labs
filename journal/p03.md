# P03: one conductor, three spellings, one vendor

The lab is [Cat-M MQTT node with GNSS stamp](../chapters/03-cat-m-mqtt-node-with-gnss-stamp.md).
Its record is mostly about a documentation hazard rather than an error, and it is
the reason a convention in this volume that looks like padding is not.

## The reading, Saturday 3 October 2026

This board was read after P02's withdrawal, to find out whether it made the same
claim. It does not. The SIM7070G leaves the draft's indicator pin alone, and
puts the module's power key on header pin 7, BCM GPIO4, exactly where the
SIM7020E puts it.

Two other things it does claim are worth knowing before wiring:

| Connection | Header pin | BCM | Condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| DTR | 37 | GPIO26 | **Only** with the DTR jumper in position A. Default is disconnected |
| IO level select | jumper | | Default 3.3 V. The other position makes the HAT's logic **5 V** |

That second row is the single connection the volume's electrical safety lab
exists to prevent, offered on this board as a two-position jumper. Never move it.

## The near miss

An intermediate version of the indicator correction had used BCM26. That is the
pin the DTR jumper connects in position A, and it is also a board-address pin on
the data acquisition HAT in another lab.

**It was abandoned for the second reason, found while writing a different
chapter, before anyone had read this board's pages properly.** The right answer
arrived partly by luck. The reading is what settles it now, and the luck is
recorded because a volume that only prints its successful reasoning teaches
readers to trust reasoning that happened to work.

## The part that generalises: one pin, three spellings

The module's own hardware design document settles only half the question. It
confirms which lines the module has to route, PWRKEY on module pin 1, DTR on 3,
RI on 4, DCD on 5, CTS on 7, RTS on 8, STATUS on 66 and NETLIGHT on 52.

**Which Raspberry Pi header pin each of those reaches is the carrier board's
decision, not the module's.** A module datasheet cannot answer it and the HAT's
own pages must. That distinction is worth internalising before reading any
vendor material: a module document and a carrier document answer different
questions, and confusing them produces claims that are individually sourced and
jointly wrong.

Then the spellings. The vendor's product page calls the power key pin "P4". The
same vendor's wiki calls it "P7 (wiringPi number)". Neither page says which
numbering scheme it is using. **They are the same conductor:** wiringPi 7 is BCM
GPIO4 is physical header pin 7.

A reader who assumes physical numbering on the first page wires the power key to
the 5 V rail.

This is why every pin in this volume is given as header pin and BCM number
together, in every table, every time. It reads as padding until the first time it
is not.

## Monday 20 October 2026, 16:15 to 17:00: the UART answers, and not from this HAT

The bearer question is still open and this sitting did not close it. What it
did establish is worth the entry, and one of the two findings is a mistake
made at the bench rather than a property of any part.

**Both modem HATs were on the one header.** The SIM7600E-H was seated and the
SIM7070G was stacked on top of it, which puts two module transmit pins on
GPIO15 and two `PWRKEY` lines on GPIO4. Two push-pull outputs driving one
input is contention, not merely ambiguity, and this chapter already carries
the rule as a pitfall in its own words. The Pi was powered down and the
SIM7600E-H removed as soon as the photographs showed it.

**The module that answered `/dev/serial0` was the SIM7600E-H.** That matters
more than the stacking, because the reading looked like a result. `AT` and
`ATE1` returned `OK` on the Pi's own UART, which is the route this chapter
documents and which had never been tested. Three readings together named the
responder. `AT+CMNB=?` returned `ERROR`, and a SIM7070G cannot refuse the
bearer selector it exists for. `AT+CNMP=?` returned
`(2,9,10,13,14,19,22,38,39,48,54,59,60,63,67)`, identical value for value to
what the SIM7600E-H had answered on `ttyUSB2` an hour earlier, and carrying
CDMA, EVDO and TD-SCDMA modes that a Cat-M module's table does not. And
`+CME ERROR: SIM not inserted`, from the HAT whose SIM had just been moved
into the other one. Had the bearer commands been run and written down from
that port, this volume would now record a SIM7070G refusing Cat-M, which is
false.

**So the UART route works and the chapter can stop calling the HAT's USB
cable the only way in.** `/dev/serial0` points at `ttyS0`, `enable_uart=1` is
in `config.txt` so the mini UART's baud is pinned to a fixed core clock,
no login shell holds the port, and ModemManager filters `ttyS0` as a
platform-driver port and does not compete for it. All four of those were
checked this evening.

**With the SIM7070G alone on the header it does not answer.** Keystrokes
reach the UART, confirmed with minicom's local echo, and nothing comes back,
at 115200. Everything that could be eliminated from the host side was:
`PWR` steady and `NET` blinking, so the module is running and on a network;
`enable_uart=1` present; no `ttyUSB*` and no getty, so nothing else holds the
port. What is left is the routing of the module's own UART, which on these
HATs is a jumper block choosing between the on-board USB bridge and the
40-pin header, and that block's legend has not been read. It is the next
thing to do and it is one photograph.

**The bench is left this way.** The SIM7070G alone on eplepi's header, the
Elisa prepaid in its holder, its LTE antenna on the `LTE` port and the `GNSS`
port empty. The SIM7600E-H is off the board, with no SIM. The
`elisa-lte` NetworkManager connection is defined and **down**, which is where
a roaming prepaid should be left; `sudo nmcli connection up elisa-lte` brings
it back, and it will want the SIM returned to the SIM7600E-H first.

## Monday 20 October 2026, 19:30 to 21:00: the Pi 3's serial port, fixed and measured

The lab moved to the host its own chapter names, the Raspberry Pi 3 Model B
Plus running this bench's Yocto image, hostname `raspberrypi3-64`, kernel
`6.6.63-v8`, BusyBox userland. The modem still says nothing. The host no
longer does, and that is the result of the sitting.

**The header UART on that board had never worked, and now does.** The bench
notes have carried it as unexplained since Tuesday 6 October 2026. Three
readings named it from three directions. `uart0_pins/brcm,pins` decodes to
GPIO30, 31, 32 and 33, which is the Bluetooth link, so the PL011 points
inside the Pi rather than at the header. GPIO14 and GPIO15 belong to the
mini UART on a Pi 3, and the mini UART was the device printing
`there is not valid maps for state default`. The pinmux agreed:
`(MUX UNCLAIMED)` on both pins. Two lines fixed it, in that image's boot
configuration, both backed up as `*.before-uart-2026-10-20`:

    dtoverlay=uart1,txd1_pin=14,rxd1_pin=15        # into config.txt
    console=serial0,115200                         # removed from cmdline.txt

and one service, `serial-getty@ttyS0`, disabled, which is the same login
shell that `raspi-config` turns off on Debian and which otherwise owns the
port from boot.

**Then it was measured rather than assumed, with a jumper between physical
pins 8 and 10.** At 9600, 19200, 38400, 57600 and 115200 a 63-byte pattern
returned 63 bytes every time. A 1008-byte burst at 115200 returned 1008.
The driver's own counters read `tx:1347 rx:1347` with no framing, parity or
overrun counts. So the port is not merely muxed, it carries data at every
rate this volume's modems use, and without losing a byte on a reply longer
than `OK`, which matters because the mini UART has no flow control.

**With the SIM7070G seated alone, the port stays silent.** `tx` rises, `rx`
collects nothing but NUL bytes, and those are not data: with nothing being
sent, the line produces about 40 spurious bytes in three seconds at 115200
and 3 at 9600, a rate that scales with the baud rate, which is what a
floating input framed by noise does. The large counts appeared only while
the Pi was transmitting, which is our own switching on pin 8 coupling into
an undriven neighbour on pin 10. A line genuinely held low would deliver
about 34,000 bytes in three seconds of quiet.

**A bias test says the header pins have nothing on them.** Claiming each pin
briefly as an input and reading it under an internal pull-down, then under a
pull-up, with an unconnected pin as a control:

    GPIO14  pd:0 pu:1      GPIO15  pd:0 pu:1
    GPIO4   pd:0 pu:1      GPIO26  pd:0 pu:1
    GPIO23  pd:0 pu:1      (control, nothing attached)

Every HAT pin behaves exactly like the control. GPIO4 is the telling one:
its path into the HAT is passive, through H3 and R1 into a transistor's
base-emitter junction to ground, so it loads a pulled-up input whether or
not the HAT has power. Earlier in the sitting it did read `pd:0 pu:0`, a
hard clamp, and after the supply was changed it stopped. **So the contact
changed, and the module's state did not.** Whether the HAT's 40-pin
connector is making contact at all is the open question, and the test for it
is to unplug the micro-USB from the HAT and see whether its LEDs die, since
that cable also carries 5 V and the LEDs therefore prove nothing about
seating on their own.

**The chapter's USB route cannot work on this image at all**, which is worth
knowing before anyone repairs that socket. The rootfs ships twelve kernel
modules, under `i2c`, `iio` and `net`. There is no `usb` driver directory,
and neither `option.ko` nor `qmi_wwan` appears in `modules.dep`, although
the kernel configuration carries `CONFIG_USB_SERIAL=m` and
`CONFIG_USB_NET_QMI_WWAN=m`. They were built and never packaged. That is a
fix in the image recipe rather than at the bench.

**One supply note, because it invalidated a measurement.** The Pi's red
`PWR` LED was blinking, which on a Pi 3B+ means the 5 V rail is dipping
below about 4.65 V, and a modem's bursts on a marginal supply is this
chapter's own pitfall. The image has no `vcgencmd` and no `rpi_volt` hwmon
node, so that LED is the only instrument available there. A proper adapter
made it steady, and the bias readings above were taken again afterwards;
the first set is not quoted.

## Monday 20 October 2026, 23:18: the control, and two faults rather than one

The entry above left one question open and guessed at the answer. The
control settles it and the guess was wrong, so it is withdrawn here.

**A known-good HAT, on the Pi 3B+, is also silent.** The SIM7600E-H was
moved onto that board, alone, with no micro-USB cables, and given eight
power cycles across 25-second listening windows at five baud rates. Not one
byte arrived in either direction. Then the same HAT went back onto eplepi,
alone, and answered `AT` with `OK` on `/dev/serial0` at 115200 at 23:18.

**So there are two faults, not one, and each is now attributable to a
side.** On eplepi, a host proven by a working module tonight, the SIM7070G
transmits nothing: that is the HAT's own fault. On `raspberrypi3-64`, a
module proven by eplepi tonight transmits nothing: that is the board's
fault. Either alone would have been explained away by the other.

**What makes the Pi 3B+ result worth keeping is what it survives.** That
port was proven three independent ways before the module was ever blamed:
GPIO14 and GPIO15 muxed to the mini UART in the pinmux; 1008 bytes returned
without loss through a jumper across physical pins 8 and 10, at 9600 through
115200, with `tx` and `rx` counters equal and no framing or overrun counts;
and the absolute bit rate measured by timing a known transfer, 112993 against
a nominal 115200 and 9460 against 9600, which refutes the core-clock
hypothesis that a loopback alone could neither confirm nor deny. **The same
measurement was then run on eplepi, the host this HAT does work on, and gave
112993 and 9460 to the digit.** So the Pi 3B+'s bit rate is not merely close to
nominal, it is identical to the rate that works, and the two percent shortfall
is the timing pipeline rather than either board. The baud is eliminated as the
difference between the two hosts by measurement and not by argument. The header is mated, confirmed by the HAT's own pull-ups
holding both UART pins high against an internal pull-down. The module is
running, since this HAT's `PWR` and `3V3` are shorted by default, which
holds `PWRKEY` asserted and is why eight button presses changed neither
LED. Every software-visible property of that port is correct and a module
that works elsewhere still says nothing on it.

**What was eliminated on the way, so nobody repeats it.** The HAT's own LEDs
prove nothing about seating, because its micro-USB carries 5 V and `PWR`
reports the board's regulator rather than the module. The image ships twelve
kernel modules, none of them USB serial, so the chapter's USB route cannot
work there whatever the socket does. And the `UART_JMP` block is on `B`, the
position that routes the module to the header, which is the position that
works on eplepi.

**For the chapter this is a host problem, not a lab problem.** P03 names the
Pi 3B+ as its host, and this bench's Pi 3B+ cannot drive the HAT even with
its serial port repaired and measured. Until that is understood the lab runs
on the Pi 4, which costs nothing the chapter depends on.
